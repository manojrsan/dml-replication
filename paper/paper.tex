	\section{The Original Paper}
	
	This study replicates a specific result from \textit{Double/Debiased Machine Learning for
		Treatment and Structural Parameters} by Victor Chernozhukov, Denis Chetverikov, Mert
	Demirer, Esther Duflo, Christian Hansen, Whitney Newey, and James Robins, published in
	\textit{The Econometrics Journal} in 2018 \citep{chernozhukov2018}.
	
	The paper addresses a fundamental problem in causal inference: when the number of
	controls is large relative to the sample size, na\"ively plugging machine learning
	estimates of nuisance functions into a structural equation produces estimators that are
	biased at the $\sqrt{N}$ rate due to regularization bias and overfitting. The authors
	show that this bias can be removed by combining two ingredients: (1)
	\textit{Neyman-orthogonal} moment conditions, which have reduced sensitivity to errors
	in nuisance estimation, and (2) \textit{cross-fitting}, an efficient form of
	sample-splitting that prevents overfitting from contaminating the structural estimate.
	The resulting procedure is called Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML).
	
	\section{The Result of Interest}
	
	This replication targets the 5-fold cross-fitting row of Panel B (Partially Linear
	Regression model) of Table 2, which reports the estimated Average Treatment Effect (ATE)
	of 401(k) eligibility (\texttt{e401}) on net total financial assets (\texttt{net\_tfa})
	across six machine learning methods and an ensemble. Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:main}
	reproduces the paper's targets exactly.
	
	This particular result is the most directly interpretable in the paper: it translates
	the DML procedure into a concrete, policy-relevant estimate using a well-known dataset,
	and directly demonstrates that the choice of nuisance learner has only modest effects on
	the structural parameter once debiasing is applied correctly.
	
	\section{Data and Methods}
	
	\subsection*{Data}
	
	The data come from the 1991 Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP), following
	the sample construction of \citet{abadie2003}. The raw file \texttt{input/sipp1991.dta}
	is loaded by \texttt{code/preprocess.R}, which selects the eleven analysis variables and
	drops incomplete cases, producing a balanced sample of household-level observations saved
	to \texttt{temp/clean\_data.rds}. The outcome variable is net total financial assets
	(\texttt{net\_tfa}) and the treatment indicator is whether the household's employer offers
	a 401(k) plan (\texttt{e401}). Nine covariates are included: age, income, education,
	family size, marital status, two-earner status, defined benefit pension status, IRA
	participation, and home ownership.
	
	\subsection*{Methods}
	
	The partially linear regression (PLR) model is:
	\begin{align}
		Y &= D\theta_0 + g_0(X) + U, \quad E[U \mid X, D] = 0, \\
		D &= m_0(X) + V, \quad E[V \mid X] = 0,
	\end{align}
	where $Y$ is net financial assets, $D$ is 401(k) eligibility, $X$ is the vector of
	controls, and $\theta_0$ is the ATE of interest. The nuisance functions $g_0$ and $m_0$
	are estimated via cross-fitting with $K=5$ folds. Six learners are used for nuisance
	estimation: Lasso (with a rich polynomial and interaction feature matrix matching the
	original paper), Regression Tree, Random Forest, Boosting, Neural Network, and an
	Ensemble average. The DML2 score function is used throughout. Results are aggregated
	across 100 random sample splits, reporting the median ATE and two standard error
	measures.
	
	\section{Replication Results}
	
	Table~\ref{tab:main} presents the replicated results alongside the paper's targets.
	In plain terms, the ATEs indicate that 401(k) eligibility increases net financial assets
	by roughly \$8,000--\$9,700 depending on the learner used for nuisance estimation, a
	large and economically meaningful effect relative to the sample mean of \$18,052. The
	stability of this estimate across very different machine learning methods, from a
	penalised linear model to a neural network, directly illustrates the DML estimator's
	central claim of robustness to nuisance function specification.
	
	\input{../output/tables/main_result.tex}
	
	The replication closely matches the paper for tree-based and ensemble methods: Random
	Forest (9,269 vs.\ 9,247), Boosting (8,983 vs.\ 9,110), Ensemble (9,338 vs.\ 9,166),
	and Best (9,667 vs.\ 9,215) all fall within a few hundred units of their targets, well
	within one standard error. The Neural Network ATE of 9,213 modestly exceeds the paper's
	9,038 by approximately 175 units, likely due to random variation in weight initialisation
	across splits. The Lasso ATE of 9,676 exceeds the paper's 8,187 by approximately 1,500,
	likely reflecting the reduced polynomial degree used here (\texttt{poly(inc, 4)} versus
	\texttt{poly(inc, 8)} in the original). The two SE rows capture different uncertainty
	sources: the bracket row $[\cdot]$ adds split-to-split variability on top of the
	within-split SE reported in the parenthesis row $(\cdot)$; both are closely matched
	across all methods, confirming the score function and aggregation are correctly
	implemented.
	
	\section{What I Learned}
	
	The most straightforward aspect of the replication was the DML estimator itself. Once
	the cross-fitting loop and score function are correctly coded, the structural estimates
	fall out cleanly and the aggregation across 100 splits is mechanical. The framework's
	robustness to learner choice, its central theoretical claim, is also directly visible
	in the results: once nuisance functions are estimated reasonably well, $\theta_0$ is
	stable across very different ML methods, confirming the paper's key insight empirically.
	
	The hardest part was matching the nuisance function specification. The paper's original
	\texttt{R} code contains a compact one-line formula (\texttt{xl}) for the Lasso feature
	matrix that expands to hundreds of columns of polynomials and pairwise interactions,
	but this detail does not appear in the paper text at all; it only appears in the code.
	Without reading \texttt{401K.R} carefully, it would be easy to run Lasso on the raw
	nine covariates and obtain a badly biased estimate, incorrectly concluding the
	replication failed. Computational cost was a secondary challenge: with over 5,000 total
	ML fits, runtime exceeded seven hours until Forest and GBM settings were tuned. Exact
	seed matching with the original is also not possible since the paper uses parallel
	execution with per-worker seeds. Both issues point to the same lesson: code-level
	documentation matters as much as methodological description. If I were the original
	author, I would explicitly state the feature construction for penalised learners and
	the parallelisation setup in a replication appendix.
